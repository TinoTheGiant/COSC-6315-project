\begin{abstract}
Distributed databases decide how to split their data and where to put the
pieces once, when the database is designed, using a function of the key that
never sees where requests come from. When the geographic centre of a workload
moves, as it does daily in a service spread across regions, that placement
drifts out of step with the traffic and transactions get slower. This paper
states continuous re-splitting and re-placement as a reinforcement learning
problem over a simulated six-region cluster. On a real six-node CockroachDB
cluster, gathering primary copies in the region issuing the transactions
rather than spreading them changes 95th percentile latency by 1372.5\,ms, so
the premise holds outside simulation. Sweeping how fast the workload moves
shows a simple reactive rule 0.44\,ms above the best available placement under
slow drift and 57.20\,ms above it under fast drift, which is 41\% of its own
latency; at that setting the rule stops beating fixed placement at all once
movement and imbalance are counted. A Deep Q-Network trained there beats both
baselines on held-out seeds, on two training seeds, by moving one twentieth of
the data the rule moves. The margin is narrow and the policy had not
converged, so this is evidence that the room the sweep identifies is
reachable, not that the problem is solved.
\end{abstract}
