\section{Evaluation}

The evaluation answers four questions, in order. First, does it actually
matter on real hardware where a shard's leaseholder sits, or is the whole
premise an artefact of the simulator? Second, is the gain from moving data
a gain from \emph{choosing} where to move it, rather than from movement by
itself? Third, how does the room available to a better policy change as the
workload moves faster? Fourth, does the learned policy use that room.

The first three have clean answers. The fourth has a partial one, and this
section reports the partial answer rather than the one that would read
better.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Setup}

The simulated cluster has six regions, one node per region, replication
factor three, and a keyspace divided into 256 cells that shards are built
from. An episode is 200 decision windows. At each window the policy sees
the per-region read and write counts from the window just finished and
issues one action; the environment then charges it for the latency its
placement produced and for the data any move had to carry.

Every policy is driven by the same episode loop. The learned policy
satisfies the same interface as the baselines and is executed by the same
rollout function, so a difference between two numbers in this section
cannot be a difference between two pieces of harness code. The baselines
are static hash partitioning, random relocation, a reactive heuristic that
follows the traffic, and a per-window bound.

The bound deserves a sentence of its own, because it is easy to misread. It
is the lowest latency reachable by relocating every lease for free, each
window, \emph{at the shard boundaries the episode started with}. It is not
a global optimum. A policy that splits a shard can legitimately score below
it, so the figures label it ``best per window (fixed shards)'' rather than
``optimal''.

The agent minimises a single cost, which is the thing it is being judged on
throughout this section:
%
\begin{equation}
c = \frac{\ell}{100} \;+\; w \cdot \frac{g}{2000} \;+\; 0.10 \cdot b
\label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}
%
where $\ell$ is mean transaction latency in milliseconds, $g$ is gigabytes
moved over the episode, $b$ is load imbalance, and $w$ is the migration
weight. In plain terms: latency is the thing we want low, moving data is
charged for, and concentrating the whole workload onto one node is charged
for separately so that a policy cannot win on latency by overloading a
single region. Migration cost is expressed as a fraction of the dataset
rather than in bytes, which is deliberate: a 60\,GB simulator and a 1\,GB
cluster then score the same physical move identically, and a policy trained
in one can be evaluated in the other without rescaling the reward.

Two seed blocks appear in this section and they are never mixed in one
table. The volatility sweep uses seeds 0 to 7. The agent evaluations use
held-out seeds 2000 to 2007, which the training configuration refuses to
overlap with its training seeds. A sweep number and an agent number are
therefore not directly comparable, and every table states which block it
came from.

Each run is identified by the first eight hexadecimal digits of a SHA-256
digest over its resolved configuration. The digest is reconstructible from
the parameters alone, which is how the weights behind the runs in
Table~\ref{tab:agent} were recovered and confirmed without reopening the
archives.

The cluster experiments use six CockroachDB nodes in containers with
inter-region round-trip latency injected by \texttt{netem}, accurate to
0.1\,ms across the measured pairs. Both pilots ran on GitHub Actions
runners, the first on two cores and the second on four.

\subsection{Q1: placement changes latency on real hardware}

Table~\ref{tab:cluster} and Fig.~\ref{fig:cluster} report two pilots on the
real cluster. In each one the same transactions run twice: once with the
leaseholders for the keys involved gathered in the region issuing them, and
once with those leaseholders spread across regions.

                  & spread     & 1013.4 & 1648.0 & 302.0 \\
\addlinespace

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{cluster.png}
\caption{Co-located against spread leaseholders on the real cluster. The
difference is large enough that it is not a measurement artefact, which is
all this experiment is asked to establish.}
\label{fig:cluster}
\end{figure}

The p95 gap is 1372.5\,ms in the first pilot and 1202.0\,ms in the second.
Placement is worth a great deal on real hardware, which is the assumption
the rest of the work rests on.

Three qualifications belong with that number rather than in a footnote.

First, ``co-located'' means the leases, not the data. With replication
factor three and one node per region, three replicas cannot fit inside one
region, so every write still pays a cross-region quorum even in the
co-located case. That is why the co-located p50 is 272.7\,ms rather than
single digits. What the experiment isolates is the leaseholder effect,
which is precisely what the \textsc{move\_lease} action corresponds to.

Second, the interval around p95 is indicative rather than a precision
claim. At $N=200$ transactions the p95 is the 190th order statistic, so a
bootstrap interval over it is degenerate at the top. The second pilot
raised $N$ to 1000 and halved the interval width from 155.2\,ms to
65.6\,ms, which is the right direction, but choosing $N$ by measurement
rather than by convenience remains outstanding work.

Third, and least comfortable, the co-located arm became 62\% slower when
the pilot moved from two cores to four, 272.7\,ms to 441.5\,ms, whilst the
spread arm barely moved. The co-located case concentrates every lease on
one node, and on the larger run that node saturated. The simulator cannot
reproduce this, because its cost model has no notion of a node running out
of capacity. It is also the clearest evidence in the project that the
imbalance term in~\eqref{eq:objective} is measuring something real rather
than something invented to make the reward well behaved.

\subsection{Q2: the gain comes from choosing, not from moving}

\begin{table}[t]
\caption{Volatility sweep. Mean latency in milliseconds and gigabytes moved
per episode, eight seeds per value, seed block 0 to 7. Source:
\texttt{results/recorded/sweep.csv}.}
\label{tab:sweep}
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lrrrrr}
\toprule
Volatility & Static & Random & Follow & Bound & Follow GB \\
\midrule
0.5 & 156.27 & 156.79 &  94.31 & 93.87 &  405.1 \\
1.0 & 156.11 & 158.02 &  98.66 & 93.66 &  785.2 \\
2.0 & 155.37 & 158.89 & 125.37 & 89.47 & 1056.6 \\
4.0 & 155.77 & 160.24 & 139.58 & 82.38 & 1115.2 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The random baseline exists to separate two explanations that are easy to
confuse. It relocates shards at the same rate as the heuristic but to
destinations chosen without looking at the traffic. Across all four
volatility settings in Table~\ref{tab:sweep} it moves 204.8\,GB and ends up
between 156.79 and 160.24\,ms, which is no better than doing nothing at
all. The heuristic, at volatility 1.0, moves 785.2\,GB and reaches
98.66\,ms, a 36.8\% improvement on static hashing.

So movement by itself buys nothing. Everything in this problem is in the
choice of destination. That is worth stating plainly because it is the
reason the problem is a decision problem at all, and therefore the reason a
learned policy is a sensible thing to attempt.

\subsection{Q3: the room for a better policy grows with volatility}

Volatility controls how fast the geographic centre of the workload drifts
between windows. Table~\ref{tab:sweep} and Fig.~\ref{fig:sweep} sweep it
over a factor of eight. Three things happen at once, and the third is the
finding.

Static hashing is flat, 155.37 to 156.27\,ms across the whole sweep. It
does not degrade as the workload moves faster because it was never tracking
the workload in the first place. Anything it does badly, it does badly at
every setting.

The bound \emph{improves} as volatility rises, 93.87\,ms down to 82.38\,ms.
This is initially counter-intuitive and worth a sentence. A more volatile
workload is a more sharply concentrated one, and a sharply concentrated
workload is easier to serve well if you put the data in the right place.
Faster drift therefore makes the problem harder for a policy and easier for
an oracle.

The heuristic goes the other way. Its latency rises from 94.31 to
139.58\,ms whilst the data it moves rises from 405.1 to 1115.2\,GB
(Fig.~\ref{fig:movement}). It pays nearly three times as much and gets
progressively less for it. A rule that reacts to the window just finished
is chasing a target that has already moved on, and under fast drift most of
what it buys with that movement has expired by the time the move lands.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{sweep.png}
\caption{Left: latency against volatility for static hashing, the reactive

opens from 0.44\,ms to 57.20\,ms. Seed block 0 to 7.}
\label{fig:sweep}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{movement.png}
\caption{Data moved per episode. The heuristic's bill rises with volatility
whilst its advantage falls, which is the behaviour a learned policy has to

\label{fig:movement}

The consequence is the right-hand panel of Fig.~\ref{fig:sweep}. The gap
between the heuristic and the bound is 0.44\,ms at volatility 0.5 and
57.20\,ms at volatility 4, which is 41\% of the heuristic's own latency.
Put simply: under slow drift a reactive rule is already close to the best
anyone could do, and there is nothing worth learning. Under fast drift
there is a great deal worth learning. The case for a learned policy is
conditional on volatility, and that condition is now measured rather than
assumed.

This also corrects an earlier reading of our own data. From the volatility
1.0 results alone, where the gap is 5.00\,ms, we concluded that latency was
not a viable contribution and that the work would have to argue from
movement cost instead. The sweep shows that conclusion was drawn from a
single point on a curve that had not been plotted.

\subsection{Q4: the learned policy wins at high volatility, narrowly}

2007, each scored against the baselines under~\eqref{eq:objective} at that
run's own migration weight. Fig.~\ref{fig:objective} shows the same

The agent beats static hashing in all five runs. At volatility 4 with
$w=0.30$ it also beats the heuristic, on both learner seeds, by 0.068 and
0.031 of objective cost. It does so in a way that is easy to describe: it
reaches 139.91\,ms against the heuristic's 129.04\,ms, so it is slower, but
it moves 62.7\,GB against 1086.5\,GB and holds imbalance at 0.776 against
1.0034. It is buying its latency far more cheaply than the heuristic buys
its own.

Fig.~\ref{fig:objective} shows a second result that falls out of the same
scoring. At volatility 4 the heuristic and static hashing are
indistinguishable under the objective, 1.554 against 1.557, despite the
heuristic being 21\,ms faster. Everything it gains on latency it returns in
movement and imbalance. The reactive rule has stopped being worth running
at all by that point, which is a sharper statement of Q3's finding than the
latency curve alone can make.

Four qualifications, and they are substantial.

\emph{The policy has not converged.} Held-out evaluation was still
improving at the final training step in every run. The training budget ran
out before the learning did, so these are lower bounds on what this
architecture reaches, not its ceiling.

\emph{No claim about the migration weight is supportable from this data.}
The two learner seeds at volatility 4 differ by 4.70\,ms, whilst changing
$w$ from 0.30 to 0.10 at a fixed seed changes latency by 2.97\,ms. The
spread between seeds is larger than the effect of the treatment, so runs 3,
4 and 7 cannot be read as telling us what $w$ should be. More seeds per
setting is the fix, and it has not been done.

\emph{Most of the agent's actions do nothing.} Between 80\% and 90\% of the
actions it selects are inapplicable in the state it selected them in, which
the environment resolves as a no-op. The random baseline sits at 80.4\% on
the same measure, so the learned policy is barely better than chance at
choosing an action that is even legal. It is winning on the actions that do
land, and it is wasting most of its decisions. This is the single largest
measured inefficiency in the project.

\emph{The bound is still far away.} At volatility 4 the agent scores 1.486
against the bound's 0.813. Beating a heuristic that has itself stopped
working is a much weaker result than closing the gap to what is achievable,
and Fig.~\ref{fig:objective} is drawn so that the remaining distance is
visible rather than cropped out.

\subsection{Threats to validity}

The simulator's cost model is analytic. Q1 establishes that placement
matters on real hardware; it does not establish that the simulator's
magnitudes are right, and no claim in this paper should be read as if it
did.

The two pilots differ in both transaction count and core count, so the
difference between them cannot be attributed to either one alone. They are
reported as two separate pilots for that reason.

$N$ was chosen by convenience in the first pilot and raised in the second
without a power calculation behind either value.

The bound holds the starting shard boundaries fixed, so it is not a global
optimum and a policy that splits shards may legitimately pass it.

The high rate of inapplicable actions is a known failure mode as the
invalid share of an action space grows \cite{huang2022masking}, and
action-elimination networks built on DQN address it directly
\cite{zahavy2018elimination}. Neither is implemented here.

Results at one volatility should not be generalised to another, which is
exactly what Q3 demonstrates. The agent results at volatility 4 say nothing
about volatility 1, and the project's own earlier misreading is the
cautionary case.
